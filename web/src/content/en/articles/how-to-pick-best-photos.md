The fastest way to pick your best photos from hundreds of shots is to decide the final number first, then cut down in passes. The first pass only removes obvious failures. Later passes compare similar frames side by side until you reach the target. You never have to decide which photo is "the best" in one go. Each photo only needs one answer: keep it or not.

This method works with any tool, even plain File Explorer. The last section shows how Pulahpilih, a free photo culling app for Windows, puts it into practice.

## Why culling photos is tiring

Culling is hard not because of the number of photos, but because of the number of decisions. Every frame asks you to weigh focus, expression, composition and how it compares with the frames around it. After a few hundred decisions your standards drift. A photo you rejected ten minutes ago suddenly looks fine, or the other way round.

Three habits make it worse:

- **No target.** Without a final number you don't know when to stop, so you keep too much.
- **Judging photos in isolation.** A good frame only looks good next to its neighbours.
- **Trying to be perfect in the first pass.** Deciding too finely too early makes the whole job slow and draining.

## What photographers look for

Agree on your criteria before you start. Order matters: technical checks are quick and remove the most photos.

### Technical checks

- **Sharp focus where it matters.** Usually the eyes in portraits. Missed focus rarely survives editing.
- **Eyes open.** In group shots, check every face. One blink often sinks the whole frame.
- **Usable exposure.** RAW files leave plenty of room, but clipped whites and blacks stay clipped.
- **No major distractions.** Someone walking through, a hand over a face, a limb cut at an awkward point.

### Storytelling checks

- **Expression and moment.** A real laugh, the right glance, a movement at its peak.
- **Composition.** Does your eye land on the subject straight away?
- **Variety.** Out of ten similar frames, keep one or two. A set full of near-duplicates feels flat.

## A pass-by-pass culling method

Split the work into passes, each with a narrower question.

1. **Set the target.** Write the number down: 50 for an album, 20 for social media, 10 for prints. A target gives you permission to reject.
2. **Pass one: drop the obvious misses.** Use the technical checks only. Don't linger; if in doubt, keep it for now. The goal is fewer photos, not the best ones.
3. **Pass two: compare side by side.** View the survivors two or three at a time. Bursts and repeated poses are much easier to judge this way. Keep the strongest, reject the rest.
4. **Repeat until you're close to the target.** Each pass is slightly stricter. Two to four passes are usually enough.
5. **If you undershoot, revisit the last rejects.** When the count drops below the target, bring back a few photos from the last pass. They already survived every earlier pass, so they are solid choices.

### Avoiding decision fatigue

- **Keep sessions to roughly 30–45 minutes,** then take a break. Tired decisions are inconsistent ones.
- **Use the keyboard, not the mouse.** One key to keep and one to reject beats clicking and dragging.
- **Don't edit while culling.** Choosing and editing are separate jobs. Edit only what survives.
- **Sort by capture time.** Frames from the same moment end up next to each other, which makes comparing easy.
- **Zoom only when it matters.** Check sharpness when two frames look equally good, not for every photo.

## How Pulahpilih applies this method

[Pulahpilih](/en) is built around exactly this workflow. It is free, open source and runs on Windows 10 and 11.

- **You set the target up front:** a folder and the number of photos you want to end with.
- **Three photos are shown at once,** side by side or stacked. The left or top one is being judged; the other two are there for comparison.
- **One key per decision.** → or ↑ picks, ← or ↓ rejects, Backspace undoes the last decision, and Space or Z zooms in to check focus.
- **Rounds run on their own.** In the first round, picks move into a `selected` subfolder. If you still have more than the target, the next round shows only `selected`, and rejects go back to the original folder. If you have fewer, the photos you rejected in the last round come back until the count is exact.
- **Choose the order:** name, date taken (EXIF), date modified, file size or random.
- **Nothing is ever deleted.** Photos only move between the original folder and `selected`. Close the app mid-way, open the same folder later, and it picks up where you left off.

## FAQ

### How many photos should I keep from one event?

There is no fixed number. Work back from what the photos are for: the number of album pages, the slots in a post, or what the client asked for. That number is your target.

### What is photo culling?

Culling is the photographer's term for filtering a shoot before editing: removing the misses and keeping the frames worth working on.

### Should I cull before or after editing?

Before. Editing photos you will throw away is wasted time. Cull down to your target first, then edit only the keepers.

### What if two photos look equally good?

Zoom in on both and check focus on the eyes and small details like the mouth and brows. If they are still level, keep the one with the cleaner composition. You can always undo a decision later.
